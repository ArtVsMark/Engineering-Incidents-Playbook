# A role is created if it can object, not merely add

**Area.** roles

**The rule.** A new role passes an admission test: its own question, its own
artefact, **its own objection** to a specific existing role. If it fails, it is
a documentation section, not a role.

## The incident

A list of roles tends to become a dump of competencies: every new consideration
wants to be its own specialist. The cost is not abstract — the role-selection
block lives in the main file and enters the context of **every** request, and a
policy of "when in doubt, engage them all" means each new role lengthens every
broad answer.

So a test appeared: write three lines in which the new role **objects** to a
named existing one. If all you can produce is "agreed, let me add a detail",
there is no role.

The test filtered out exactly what had seemed obvious. "Business analyst"
dissolved between three existing roles. "Systems analyst" turned out to be a
pair of existing roles plus the decision-record practice. "Reporting analyst"
failed on the artefact: there is no telemetry and none is planned, so it has
nothing to read.

Separately it emerged that **the axis of division** matters. Two documentation
roles work when divided by audience and quality criterion: one writes for the
developer, its criterion is accuracy against the code; the other writes for
somebody outside the context, its criterion is whether they finish reading. They
argue substantively: "you shortened it and lost the caveat" against "exhaustive
and unreadable", and both objections are sometimes right.

The same pair divided along "structure versus prose" immediately begins
repeating itself: both halves answer one question — is it clear and is it
correct. That split was rejected and marked as not to be reopened.

## Why

A role is valuable for its **angle of view**, not for its volume of knowledge.
An angle exists only if it shows something the others do not — and that is tested
in exactly one way: by disagreement. A role that always agrees adds no
perspective, only length.

Second: a role with no artefact has nothing to be measured against. The question
"what does it own in the repository" filters out roles invented by analogy with
other people's teams.

Third: narrowness is not a flaw but the source of value. A specialist with a
sharp lens ("tracks every change in the language standard") stops being a
specialist the moment they also start tracking the neighbouring standard. Roles
are not created "per language" or "per technology" — only per real layer of
work.

## In practice

- three conditions, all mandatory: its own question · its own artefact · its own
  objection;
- the admission test is **three written lines of argument**, not a discussion of
  usefulness;
- a rejected role is recorded together with the reason: otherwise it will be
  proposed again;
- revisiting the axis of division is also a decision: "we split along the wrong
  axis" happens more often than "we failed to split".

## Where it applies

**Works** for sets of roles, review checklists, audit slice templates —
anything engaged from a list.

**Does not work** for a one-off line-up for a specific task: there it is cheaper
to assign than to certify.

**Sign of trouble:** roles in a report say the same thing in different words.
