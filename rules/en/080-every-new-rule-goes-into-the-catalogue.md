# A rule born in a project is recorded in the shared catalogue

**Area.** process, catalogue

**The rule.** Every hard-won new rule goes into this catalogue — with its
incident, its reason and its limits. The project keeps the working wording and a
link; the history lives here.

## The incident

Rules are born one at a time and in the middle of work: something broke, we
worked it out, we wrote a line into the project's rulebook, we moved on. The line
takes effect — and that is all.

After that one of two things happens, and both are bad.

**The rule loses its reason.** A month later the wording remains and the history
does not. A new contributor arrives, sees a restriction, does not see the
breakage it was paid for, and proposes removing it as redundant. There is nothing
to argue with: the reasoning was in a discussion, and the discussion scrolled
away.

**The rule does not travel to the next project.** A new repository starts from a
clean rulebook, and the same rakes are stepped on again — at the same cost in
time, now with the added feeling that this has happened before.

That is exactly why the catalogue appeared: not as a memo but as **the foundation
of the next projects**. It is useless if it is filled in after the fact and
selectively.

## Why

A rule has two parts, and they live in different places. **The wording** is a
working instruction; it belongs where people act on it, and it must be short.
**The history** — incident, reasoning, rejected options, limits of applicability
— has no place in the project's rulebook, where it only bloats it, yet without it
the wording is defenceless.

The catalogue also solves a third problem — **a portability test**. Writing a
rule for somebody else's project forces you to answer where it does not work.
Half the rules fail that question and turn out to be local settings rather than
rules, which is useful to learn immediately.

Recording must happen **while the trail is warm**. A rule written a month later
keeps its wording and loses exactly what the catalogue exists for: the numbers,
the sequence of events, what had been tried before.

## In practice

- the record is made **in the same pass** as the edit to the project's rulebook,
  not "later";
- the project's rulebook keeps the wording and a link to the record, not a
  retelling: two sources mean one of them is stale;
- the record must contain a specific incident and a section on where the rule
  does **not** work; without those it is a slogan;
- a rule with no incident behind it is not a rule but a preference, and does not
  enter the catalogue;
- a revised rule is not rewritten after the fact: a change is a new record, and
  the old one is marked superseded.

## Where it applies

**Works** when there is more than one project, or another is expected.

**Does not work** for one-off work with no continuation — there the catalogue
outlives the project and nobody reads it.

**Sign of a breach:** the project's rulebook contains a restriction and nobody
can say where it came from.
